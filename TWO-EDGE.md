# A restriction on two-edge deletion–contraction searches

Let $S$ be a tree with two specified edges. Deleting both gives three components. Call the component incident to both removed edges $T$ and the other two components $A,B$. The two-edge expansion of the chromatic symmetric function consists of the all-deleted forest, the two forests given by deleting one edge and contracting the other, and the tree given by contracting both edges. Contracting vertices adds their weights, and every original vertex has weight one. Matching these four weighted graphs by isomorphism, with the two mixed terms unordered, would suffice to match the chromatic symmetric functions. [Weighted deletion and contraction](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11859)

**Proposition.** If $T$ is isomorphic to neither $A$ nor $B$, the four weighted graphs determine $S$ and the unordered pair of specified edges up to isomorphism. Any non-isomorphic pair produced by matching these four terms must therefore repeat the central component type among the two outer components.

In each mixed term the component with all weights one is one of the two outer components. The mixed terms thus give the multiset of outer types $\{A,B\}$, and removing these two types from the all-deleted forest gives $T$. Removing the all-unit companion of a mixed term from the all-deleted forest gives the deletion forest of its joined pair. Its contraction has the merged vertex marked by weight two. The single-edge reconstruction theorem recovers the joined pair and its joining edge. Since $T$ differs from each outer component, its endpoint and the outer endpoint are distinguishable by component type. Consequently, each mixed term determines one outer rooted component and the individual orbit of its attachment point in $T$. [Single-edge reconstruction theorem](ROOT-EXCHANGE.md)

The double contraction determines the distance between the two attachment points in $T$. If the points coincide it has a vertex of weight three. Otherwise it has two vertices of weight two, and their distance is the original distance in $T$ because all grafted branches meet $T$ at a single vertex each.

In any finite tree an ordered pair of vertices lies in an orbit determined by the individual vertex orbits and their distance. To see this, root the tree at its center. If the center is an edge, subdivide it and root at the new vertex. The individual orbit determines the sequence of rooted descendant types on the path from the root to the vertex. It also determines the depth. In the subdivided case, the original distance and the two original distances to the nearer endpoints of the center edge determine whether the path crosses that edge. Their parity agrees with the sum of those two distances on a same-side path and differs on an opposite-side path. Thus the distance in the subdivided tree is determined as well. The depths and the rooted-tree distance determine the depth of the least common ancestor. Match the common initial paths to that depth using the common sequence of descendant types. At the least common ancestor, match the two distinct children leading to the remaining paths by their types. If the children have the same type, match them by a permutation within that class. Repeat along the two paths and complete the isomorphism on the other children by their types. If one endpoint is the least common ancestor, only one path remains. This constructs the required automorphism and descends to the original tree if the center was subdivided.

Match the two mixed terms across the expansions to order the attachment points consistently. The individual orbits and the distance now give a single automorphism of $T$ taking both points to their counterparts. Together with the two rooted outer isomorphisms, it gives an isomorphism of the full trees carrying the pair of specified edges to its counterpart.

## Search with three identical components

The exact search enumerated 3,302,297 attachment arrangements on three copies of each unlabeled tree of orders 2–12. It found 2,981,858 distinct expansions and no matching expansion on non-isomorphic joined trees. The two joining edges are the only edges that split off a component of one copy's order. An internal cut in an outer copy leaves a smaller component, and an internal cut of the central copy either leaves a smaller component or puts more than a full copy on each side. Thus the source isomorphism test may use the central path and the two rooted outer components, up to reversal of the path. A separate implementation using direct graph deletion and weighted contraction matched the counts for all 2,484 unfiltered attachments at component orders 2–5. [Search log](two-edge-repeats.jsonl)
